TAKTO ONE companion app: UI design exploration, designs 31 to 50
================================================================

This file is append-only. Once a design is committed its entry is frozen.
Mistakes are corrected in the next design, never covered up in a previous one.

LESSONS FROM 1-30
-----------------
What worked. One stage, one subject: the hand alone, nothing drawn on top of
it, and the detail hidden until asked for. The honest feed badge on every
screen. Light tabular numerals for the summary. The decision that the accent
is spent on state, not on decoration. The capture harness: a pinned clock and
a URL that pins the screen, so every still is reproducible.

What did not. The twin was flat: roughness 0.92, no clearcoat, no specular,
no contact shadow, and the ACES transform on top of a matte tone turned every
colour into the same dusty toy plastic. Thirty looks were thirty colour swaps
of that one recipe, and none of them changed the light, the lens or the
stage. The hand was also framed too small and too high, floating in a black
field with nothing to relate to. The chrome was weak: glass at 7 percent
white over black is a grey smudge, the round icon buttons could be from any
app, the picker chips were generic Feather icons in pills, the nav a three
icon capsule. Nothing said TAKTO. Nothing asked to be touched.

What the twin needs to read as a precise instrument on a phone. A lens, not
a game camera: 24 to 32 degrees, close, the hand filling at least half the
short axis. Real specular behaviour: a clearcoat or a metal, a roughness in
the 0.3 to 0.55 band, an environment to reflect, and a rim light separating
the silhouette from the stage. Material contrast INSIDE the object: the
finger members in one material, the encoder boards and pins in a second, the
palm block in a third, so the mechanism reads as assembled parts rather than
as a single cast blob. A ground relationship: a contact shadow, a reflection,
a grid, a horizon, or a paper edge, so the hand has somewhere to be. Motion
that means something: the twin moves because the joints moved.

What I will do differently. Every design starts from the twin: material,
light, camera and stage are decided and looked at before any chrome is
drawn. Every design changes stage, type, colour, layout AND navigation, not
one of them. Controls are drawn as objects a hand recognises (a key, a
dial, a toggle, a rail, a tab of paper, a bezel) with real contrast and a
real pressed state, never a translucent pill with an icon in it. Fonts are
chosen per design and Inter is not used again. Every still is read at full
size before it is committed.

---- DESIGN 31: Rams panel ----
Thesis: A machine hand is an instrument, so show it the way Braun showed a radio: in a window cut into warm grey enamel, with keys you could find with your eyes closed.
Twin: Satin white members (roughness 0.34-0.40, clearcoat 0.5) with dark steel pins and graphite encoder boards, so the mechanism reads as assembled parts. A softbox environment high left, one hard warm key with a 2048 shadow map, a cool strip rim behind right, a floor bounce. The hand stands on a shadow-catching ground in a recessed window the colour of the panel, one step darker, with an inset shade along its top edge. A 27 degree lens at 1.9 units, elevation 30, and the new fitAspect rule so a short window moves the camera in rather than losing the hand. It reads as an instrument because it is photographed like a product: one key, one rim, one shadow, and its own contrasting parts.
UI: A panel below the window. A status strip with an amber LED and the words SYNTHETIC FEED on every screen. Keys with a face, a 2px side and a soft fall, that push down when pressed; the power key is charcoal with a green LED; three wide keys at the foot are the navigation. Mean flexion is a red needle on a printed scale beside a 34pt number; the four fingers and effort are segmented level meters with a red band at the top; the JOINTS key flips the panel into a 4x3 matrix of arc dials with needles that have mass (spring). Replay is a tape transport: a printed effort trace with a red playhead you drag, PAUSE as the dark key, EJECT, a ridged rotary with three detents for speed. Data prints the wire names on value tiles and the four rates as a table. Archivo throughout, small, tracked, upper case for silkscreen, tabular numerals.
Different from every previous design because: closest to the baseline in subject only. The stage is a light enamel panel, not black; nothing is translucent; the controls are physical keys, meters, needles and a rotary rather than glass pills; navigation is a key row; the type is Archivo not Inter; the twin is a white product shot with a real shadow, not a matte tone floating on black.
Why this is the best possible UI: every reading is an instrument you already know how to read (scale, meter, dial, tape), every control is a thing you already know how to press, and the hand is shown as what it is: a white precision product on a table. It ages well; nothing here depends on a trend.
What I would do better next time: The welcome leaves a band of empty panel under the keys. The dials could carry printed limits at the red band. Skip glyph keys need drawn triangles, not text characters.

---- DESIGN 32: Editorial plate ----
Thesis: The hand as a plate in a book: a printed page you read downward, a serif headline, tables with rules, and a numbered rail in the margin for navigation.
Twin: A lithograph, not a photograph. MeshToon shading with a three-step ramp (95, 175, 250) so every cylinder carries a flat highlight, a flat mid and a flat shade; a black outline drawn by an inverted hull pushed 0.36 percent of the model size along the vertex normals, added as a child of every rigged mesh so it follows the kinematics; members pale warm grey, the palm one step darker, the encoder boards near black, the pins pale. No environment, no tone mapping, no shadow: the page is the stage and the outline is what anchors the figure. A 30 degree lens at 1.6, elevation 22, six degrees of roll, and 60 percent of the feed's wrist roll performed by the whole figure so the plate breathes. It reads as a precise instrument because every edge is drawn, the way a patent drawing is.
UI: One long page with the plate pinned at the top (sticky header) and four sections under it: Cover, Live, Replay, Data. A rail down the left margin carries four numbered marks spread over the full height; the current one is inked and any one scrolls you there. Fraunces only: 44pt light headline with an italic turn, 72pt light tabular numeral for mean flexion, italic captions under every figure and table ("Fig. 1", "Table 1"), tracked capitals for labels. Controls are printed objects: black bars with a drawn arrow for the calls to action, words underlined in ink for choices, ink discs with drawn transport glyphs. Effort is an inked line; the joint table has a heavy rule and light rules; the take list is a contents page with numbers. Printer's red is spent on the playhead, a live dot and any value near its limit.
Different from every previous design because: 31 is a panel of physical keys on grey enamel with a white photographed hand; this is a paper page with a drawn hand, serif type, no keys, no window, and a scroll-and-rail navigation instead of a key row. The baseline is black glass; this has no glass and no black stage.
Why this is the best possible UI: reading numbers is reading, and a page is the most legible thing ever designed for it. The figure and its caption make the twin's provenance explicit on every screen. Nothing is chrome; everything is content.
What I would do better next time: The toon ramp could use a fourth step for the palm block. The cover headline wraps awkwardly at "hand,"; set it in two deliberate lines. Sections could carry a running page number at the foot.

---- DESIGN 33: Blueprint sheet ----
Thesis: The twin is a drawing set: a cyanotype sheet with a grid, zone marks, a title block, and index tabs on the bottom edge that are the navigation.
Twin: White line work on Prussian blue. The body wears the blue of the sheet one shade lighter (MeshStandard, roughness 0.9) so the volumes still turn; the silhouette is a white inverted hull at 0.28 percent of the model size; every crease of the CAD is drawn by EdgesGeometry at a 30 degree threshold, added as a child of each rigged mesh so the lines articulate with the joints. A 16 degree lens at 3.3 units keeps the projection nearly orthographic, the way a drawing is. No environment, no shadow. It reads as a precise instrument because it is presented as its own engineering drawing: every hole, boss and slide is a line.
UI: The screen is a sheet: an 8pt and 40pt grid, a one-pixel border, zone letters A-D across and 1-3 down the drawing, a VIEW note in the corner, a title block at the foot with TITLE, NOTE, SHEET n OF 4 and REV, and four index tabs hanging off the bottom edge that select the sheet. JetBrains Mono throughout, tracked capitals. Mean flexion is a dimension: end ticks, a line, the value in the middle. Each finger is a dimension bar with quarter ticks. Detail is TABLE 1, a ruled table; Data is TABLE 2 (channels, wire name against mechanical name) and TABLE 3 (rates). Replay's library is a revision table with LOAD callouts; the transport is a strip chart on the grid with a yellow playhead you drag. Controls are dashed callout boxes with drafting corners; the primary is filled white; pressing any of them turns it highlighter yellow.
Different from every previous design because: 31 is grey enamel with keys and a photographed white hand; 32 is a paper page with a toon drawing and a serif; this is a saturated blue field with white line work, a mono face, tables instead of lists, dimensions instead of numerals, and tabs on the sheet edge instead of a key row or a rail.
Why this is the best possible UI: the app is literally the documentation of a mechanism, and a drawing sheet is the form engineers already trust for exactly that. Everything on screen has a place a draughtsman would recognise, and the twin is finally shown with all of its detail visible instead of smoothed away by shading.
What I would do better next time: The cover's drawing could be larger. The MEASURES column wraps in Table 2; a narrower wire column would fix it. A dimension leader from the drawing to the live value would close the loop between figure and number.

---- DESIGN 34: HUD ----
Thesis: A head-up display: a hologram of the hand turning over a radar rose, readouts bracketed into the corners, and a radial dial at the foot as the navigation.
Twin: A fresnel hologram written as a ShaderMaterial: faces toward the viewer are nearly clear, faces turning away glow phosphor green, additive over black with depth writes off, so the hand is drawn by its own silhouettes and by every place one part passes in front of another. Members glow mint, pins white, the palm and encoder boards a deeper green at a lower base so the structure reads in layers. The opacity carries a slow beat and a faint fast flicker, and the whole hand turns on a slow turntable, because a projection rotates. 26 degree lens, elevation 24. It reads as a precise instrument because you see through it to the slides and pins doing the work.
UI: Black glass, one green, amber only at the limit. Corner brackets frame every readout cell; the mean flexion and effort sit top left and top right in 30pt Chakra Petch; the four fingers are ticked gauges bottom left with an effort trace bottom right. A radar rose with 72 ticks sits under the hologram. Navigation is a radial dial: three sectors on a 200 unit arc with a ticked outer scale and a needle from a hidden centre pointing at the sector you are in; tapping a sector swings the needle. Commands are bracketed cells that fill green when pressed. The joint matrix, the take list and the transport rise as dark panels with a hairline border over the lower half. Data lists every wire name over its reading and the four rates.
Different from every previous design because: 31 is enamel and keys, 32 paper and serif, 33 a blue sheet with tables; this is black with additive green light, a squared techno face, corner brackets instead of rules, and a dial instead of keys, rail or tabs. The twin is the only transparent one so far.
Why this is the best possible UI: an operator's display puts the numbers where the eyes already are, around the subject, and never over it. The hologram shows the interior of the mechanism, which no opaque material can. The dial is one gesture from anywhere.
What I would do better next time: The needle hides most of its length below the edge; a smaller radius would show more of it. The corner cells could carry sparklines. The Data panel hides the hologram almost completely.

---- DESIGN 35: Atelier ----
Thesis: A jewel on walnut: a polished gold-titanium hand in a pool of light, garamond, and one screen you swipe across and up, with no tabs and no keys.
Twin: Polished metal, which the exploration had never tried: members at metalness 0.95 and roughness 0.16 in a warm titanium gold, the palm a shade deeper, pins bright steel, encoder boards in black lacquer under a full clearcoat. A metal is only its reflections, so the environment is a built studio: a large warm softbox overhead, a tall cool strip behind right, a warm strip behind left, a dark floor and a walnut room, so every cylinder carries one warm highlight, one cool edge and dark turns between. ACES at 1.05. A low 24 degree lens at elevation 16, a slow wide swing (0.26 rad at 0.11 Hz), no ground: the hand hangs in a radial pool of light. It reads as a precise instrument because a mirror finish shows every facet of the machining and every slide as a distinct bright shape.
UI: One screen. The hand above, and beneath it three pages you swipe between (Live, Replay, Data) marked by three small diamonds; swiping up on a page opens its detail (the twelve joints as a hairline table), swiping down closes it. The welcome is a veil over the same screen: the name in 58pt Cormorant light and italic, a champagne medallion, "tap to begin". Cormorant Garamond throughout with its old-style figures; 64pt light for mean flexion, italics for every caption, tracked capitals at 10.5pt for labels. The four fingers are hairline rings carrying their initial. Controls are hairline medallions with drawn glyphs that fill champagne when pressed, and words with a gold dot beneath when chosen. The feed badge is a diamond and a word in the masthead.
Different from every previous design because: 31 to 34 all have a visible navigation structure (keys, rail, tabs, dial); this has none, only gestures and three diamonds. The twin is the first metal, the first with a built reflection studio, and the stage is the first warm one. The type is the first old-style serif with true italics at display size.
Why this is the best possible UI: the most expensive object in the app is the hand, and this treats it as one: nothing competes, every control is a hairline, and the navigation is what a hand does naturally on glass. The numbers are set like a menu at a good restaurant, which is exactly how much decoration a number needs.
What I would do better next time: The Data page is long inside a short pager and needs a taller open state. The rings could carry the flexion number on a long press. The pool of light should follow the hand's swing.

---- DESIGN 36: Clinic ----
Thesis: A medical device: porcelain on pale blue, vitals in tiles, an arc gauge with a normal band for every joint, and large sober tabs you cannot miss.
Twin: Glazed porcelain: white members at roughness 0.22 under a full clearcoat with the room environment mirrored in every curve, the palm one step greyer, navy encoder boards and steel pins for contrast. A high soft key with a 2048 shadow map casting one light shadow onto a pale blue ground, a cool fill from behind, a wide hemisphere so nothing is harsh. Neutral tone mapping. The stage is a rounded pale-blue panel with a hairline border, the way a monitor frames its waveform. It reads as a precise instrument because it looks sterilised: no colour on the object, all the colour in the boards and the pins.
UI: White ground, navy text, one calm blue, amber only for "outside the band" and green only for OK. A title bar with the screen name and two status chips (the feed, joints live). Vitals as monitor tiles: 30pt bold numbers with small caps labels; a tile tints amber when its value leaves the band. Fingers as bars with the top 18 percent drawn as an amber band; the twelve-joint detail is a grid of arc gauges (220 degrees, 7pt stroke, the band amber, a centre tick for the signed abduction). Replay is a list of session cards with a round blue play button, then a transport with a 60pt play button, a segmented speed control and a trend you drag. Data is a labelled form and two ruled tables with a status column. Tabs at the foot are 56pt with a drawn line icon and a word, the active one on a blue tint. Source Sans 3, 400/600/700.
Different from every previous design because: it is the first light UI with saturated colour, the first with a conventional tab bar (deliberately: a device UI must be learnable in seconds), and the first with tiles and gauges that carry a normal band. The twin is glazed and white where 31 was satin and 35 was metal.
Why this is the best possible UI: this is the design a clinician would sign off on. Every reading has a band, every state has a colour that means one thing, every target is a thumb wide, and nothing is decorative. Sobriety is a feature.
What I would do better next time: The porcelain reads slightly flat on pale blue; a darker rim or a cooler key would separate the fingers better. The mean-flexion tile could carry a sparkline. The Data table wraps in the Measures column.

---- DESIGN 37: Watch face ----
Thesis: A watch: a brushed steel hand inside a round dial with complications, a ticked bezel that rotates to choose the surface, and a crown on the case that is the primary control.
Twin: Brushed steel through MeshPhysicalMaterial's anisotropy at 1.0 with the brushing rotated along each member, so each highlight becomes a streak instead of a point; the palm gunmetal, the boards near black, the pins polished rose gold as the one warm note. A long thin softbox overhead and a tall cool strip behind, a black room, ACES at 0.95 so the steel stays dark between streaks. The hand sits inside a circular mask, breathing slightly rather than swaying, at 24 degrees and elevation 30. It reads as a precise instrument because brushed steel is what precise instruments are made of, and the anisotropy makes every turned surface show its axis.
UI: Black OLED. The dial fills the width: a 120-tick bezel with LIVE, REPLAY and DATA printed on it that springs round so the chosen word sits under the rose marker at 12 o'clock; a ridged crown on the case edge that starts the session, plays and pauses, connects, or opens the joints depending on where you are, with its current meaning printed beside it; steel pushers (hairline pills that fill steel when pressed) for everything else. Complications inside the face: mean flexion top left and effort top right as 270 degree arcs with tall Barlow Condensed numerals, the four fingers as subdials at 6 o'clock. Under the dial the strap carries peak, assist and joints, four finger rows, the effort trace, the take list, the transport and the data tables.
Different from every previous design because: it is the first circular composition and the first with a rotating element as navigation; the twin is the first brushed (anisotropic) metal and the first inside a mask; the type is the first condensed face. 34 also used black, but with green additive light and brackets; this is steel and white with a rose marker.
Why this is the best possible UI: a watch is the most refined instrument people wear, and its grammar (dial, bezel, crown, complications) already solves how to read several numbers at a glance and reach a mode without looking. The whole app is one dial and one crown.
What I would do better next time: The bezel labels invert when the bezel turns; they should counter-rotate. The face could show a tiny effort sparkline complication. The welcome strap is thin.

---- DESIGN 38: Concrete ----
Thesis: Brutalism: cast iron on a concrete slab under a hard sun, monumental type set straight over the image, black blocks for controls, three huge words as the navigation.
Twin: Matte near-black members (MeshStandard, roughness 0.8, a touch of metalness so the edges catch), safety orange encoder boards as the one colour, grey steel pins. A single hard sun from low left (intensity 3.2, shadow radius 1.5, 2048 map) casting one sharp shadow at 55 percent onto the slab; a faint cool fill and a hemisphere so the black is not a hole. No environment, no tone mapping, a wide 34 degree lens from 14 degrees of elevation at 1.55 units, so the hand looms. Half the feed's wrist roll is performed by the whole hand. It reads as a precise instrument the way a machine on a factory floor does: heavy, shadowed, one warning colour where the electronics are.
UI: Concrete with formwork tie holes on a 120 by 150 grid, 3pt black rules, no radius anywhere. Anton at 96pt for the mean flexion and 80pt for the clock, set in white with a hard 3pt black offset shadow straight over the twin; Libre Franklin at 11pt for the small print. Fingers are slabs: 2pt-bordered troughs with a black fill and orange past 82 percent. Effort is a black tile with a 40pt numeral; the twelve joints are a grid of black cells with concrete numerals, orange when near the limit. Controls are black blocks with concrete Anton, or outlined blocks; every one turns orange when pressed. The navigation is three 34pt words in a 64pt strip at the foot, the active one a solid black block.
Different from every previous design because: it is the first with type over the image, the first with zero radius and 3pt rules, the first wide-angle low camera, the first with a non-neutral second colour used structurally. The concrete field is a warm grey where 31 was a lighter warm grey with keys; here nothing is extruded and nothing is soft.
Why this is the best possible UI: it is honest about weight. The numbers are the biggest thing on screen because they are what you came for; the controls cannot be missed or mistaken; and the machine looks like a machine. Nothing here needs explaining.
What I would do better next time: The effort trace box is too small beside the tile. The welcome's headline crowds the tie holes. The hard shadow could fall onto a visible slab edge to give the ground a horizon.

---- DESIGN 39: Atlas ----
Thesis: A sheet from an atlas: the hand as a landform on cream paper with contours and a graticule, readings in a legend box, and a compass rose whose four points are the navigation.
Twin: The palette of a printed relief map: matte olive members, a deeper olive palm, burnt sienna encoder boards, cream pins, all MeshStandard at roughness 0.85 to 0.9 with no environment so nothing shines. The sun comes from the north-west at 48 degrees, which is the cartographer's convention for hill shading, casting a soft 22 percent shadow onto the paper; a cool fill from the south-east and a warm hemisphere. A 30 degree lens from 42 degrees up, so the hand is seen the way a map sees terrain. Fourteen contour rings, gently irregular, are drawn around the hand's position so it reads as the summit of its own landform. It reads as a precise instrument the way a surveyed hill does: the light explains the form and the contours explain the ground.
UI: Cream paper with a faint graticule, contours, a title cartouche top left (double rule, sheet name, the feed and joints in italic), and a compass rose in the bottom right corner on its own paper inset whose four points, LIVE, REPLAY, DATA and COVER, are the navigation; the current point is filled sienna. Readings live in legend boxes with a double rule border and an italic tracked title. Fingers are scale bars (alternating black and cream segments) with a sienna marker at the value. Effort is an elevation profile with 45 degree hatching under the line; in Replay the profile is the scrubber ("drag to travel") and takes are listed with a dashed route glyph. Controls are legend entries: a swatch and a word in a boxed row, the primary one inverted, any of them sienna when pressed. Alegreya throughout, italics wide-tracked for the map labels, bold for the numerals.
Different from every previous design because: the first with a generated background that relates to the object (contours around the hand); the first with a corner instrument as navigation (a rose, four points); the first matte earth palette; the first with the sun placed by a cartographic convention rather than a studio one. 32 also used paper and a serif, but as a book page with a rail and a drawn hand; this is a map with a shaded relief hand.
Why this is the best possible UI: a map is the oldest interface for reading a lot of numbers about one thing at a glance, and its parts (legend, scale, rose, cartouche) are known to everyone. The readings are where a map reader expects them, and the hand gets the light that best explains form.
What I would do better next time: The contours should be drawn from the hand's actual silhouette, not around a point. The rose inset now covers the end of long lists; the lists could end above it. The Live legend could carry a north arrow for the hand's yaw.

---- DESIGN 40: Monoline ----
Thesis: One line weight, one ink, white: the hand as a plan-view line illustration, every gauge a thin ring or arc, and a single line across the top with three nodes as the navigation.
Twin: No shading at all. The body is the white of the page (MeshBasic), the silhouettes are a thin dark inverted hull at 0.22 percent of the model size, and every hard edge is an EdgesGeometry line at 24 degrees in the same ink at 75 percent, so the drawing has exactly one line weight, like the UI. The camera faces the back of the hand square on (azimuth 180, elevation 8) through an 18 degree lens, so the hand is laid out in plan, fingers up, the way a hand is drawn in a manual. The first attempt looked straight down and found the fingers foreshortened and the camera's up vector degenerate; the fix was to face the dorsal side, not to hover above it. It reads as a precise instrument because nothing but its edges is shown, and its edges are all there.
UI: White. A 1.25pt line does everything: the rule under the head, the ring gauges (a thin track, the value as a stroked arc, a small hollow node at its end), the arc gauges with a needle line for the twelve joints, the pills (outlined, filled ink when pressed or chosen), the input underline, the chart. Navigation is one line across the top with three nodes and their names; the node you are on is filled. Manrope light at 34pt for the numerals and the headline, 10pt tracked capitals for labels. The feed badge is a hollow or filled dot beside the words.
Different from every previous design because: 33 was line work on blue with tables and tabs; this is line work on white with rings and a node line, in a humanist sans, and the only design whose view of the hand is a plan rather than a three-quarter. Nothing here is filled.
Why this is the best possible UI: reducing everything to one line makes every mark equal and every number the loudest thing on screen. The plan view is the most legible view of twelve joints: every finger is separate and every joint is on its own line.
What I would do better next time: The rings could carry their joint names inside. The plan view hides the palm's depth; a second, small side elevation would complete the drawing. Pills with only a glyph want a fixed square width.

---- DESIGN 41: Tape deck ----
Thesis: A tape deck: bronze under a tungsten lamp, VU meters with ballistics, amber phosphor counters, piano keys for the transport and three toggle switches as the navigation.
Twin: Warm bronze members (metalness 0.9, roughness 0.36), a darker bronze palm, polished brass pins, bakelite-brown encoder boards under a light clearcoat. The environment is three tungsten panels in a dark brown room, so every reflection is 2700K and every turn goes to bakelite; a warm key, an orange rim, a low brown hemisphere; ACES at 1.0. The hand sits in a rounded bakelite window over a radial pool of lamp light and sways gently. It reads as a precise instrument the way a brass movement does: the machining shows as warm streaks and the boards show as dark inlays.
UI: Bakelite (#1E1915) with cream silkscreen in Space Mono and amber phosphor numerals with a glow. Two VU meters for mean flexion and effort, four small ones for the fingers: cream faces, black scales, a red zone above 82 percent, and needles driven by a spring so they overshoot and settle. The twelve joints and the channels are counters: dark cells with amber numerals. The transport is a row of piano keys with a 3pt drop that collapses when pressed; speed is a three-position slide switch; takes are listed with a reel glyph; the scrubber is a bar tape window with a red playhead. Navigation is three toggle switches on a plate: the lever leans up and an amber LED lights on the one that is on; the welcome's POWER and LINE are the same switches.
Different from every previous design because: 31 had keys and a rotary on light enamel with a white product; this is dark bakelite with needles, phosphor and toggles and a bronze twin. 35 was the other warm dark design, but gold, serif and gestural; this is brass, mono and mechanical.
Why this is the best possible UI: a VU needle is the best display ever made for a value that moves, because its mass tells you the rate as well as the level. Everything here has a physical analogue you have already touched, and the amber says "live" better than any dot.
What I would do better next time: The Live screen leaves the bottom third empty; the finger meters could be larger. The counters could flip. The toggles could animate the others falling.

---- DESIGN 42: Blue field ----
Thesis: A colour field: International Klein Blue edge to edge, a matte white hand and its shadow, Swiss typography, and three words at the top as the navigation.
Twin: Matte white members (roughness 0.68 to 0.72, no coat, no environment), navy encoder boards, grey steel pins. One soft high key casting a long 55 percent shadow onto the field, and a hemisphere whose ground colour is the field's own blue, so the undersides of the cylinders go blue the way a white object on a blue floor does. That bounce is what makes the hand belong to the field instead of being pasted onto it. A 28 degree lens at elevation 30. It reads as a precise instrument because a white object lit by its own coloured ground is the classic still-life test of form: every turn shows.
UI: One blue, one white. Work Sans, flush left, on a 20pt margin: 72pt bold for mean flexion, 64pt for the clock, 56pt for the cover headline set on two lines. Hairline white rules at 20 percent between rows, a 2pt rule at the head of every list. Three words at the top, Live, Replay, Data, with a 3pt bar under the current one; the feed sits at the right of the same line. Controls are words in a 2pt white outline that invert to a white block with blue text when pressed or chosen; the one primary action is a white block with an arrow. Fingers are 8pt white bars on a 20 percent track; the twelve joints are a table of 24pt numerals; Data is two ruled lists.
Different from every previous design because: it is the only saturated field, the only design with exactly two colours, and the only one with text tabs as navigation. 33 was also blue, but a darker cyanotype with a grid, line work, tables and tabs on the sheet edge; here there is no line work, no grid, no boxes, and the hand is solid white with a shadow.
Why this is the best possible UI: nothing to learn and nothing to forgive. Every number is the largest thing near it, every control is a word, the hierarchy is size and weight alone, and the twin sits on the most beautiful blue there is.
What I would do better next time: The welcome's block button is the one element with a right-aligned arrow; the pattern could repeat in the lists. The chart could be taller. Transport glyphs set as characters should be drawn.

---- DESIGN 43: Daylight ----
Thesis: Open air: a chrome hand mirroring a sky gradient, and three white sheets stacked at the foot of the screen, the front one open, the others peeking, as the navigation.
Twin: A mirror finish (metalness 1, roughness 0.06) with nothing to reflect but a built sky: a vertex-coloured sphere that runs blue overhead, pale at the horizon and warm earth below, so the top of every member takes the sky and the underside takes the ground. Navy lacquered encoder boards, white chrome pins. A soft sun, a sky-and-earth hemisphere, ACES at 1.05, no shadow and no ground: the hand floats and breathes, and performs 70 percent of the feed's wrist roll. It reads as a precise instrument because chrome shows every curvature change as a change of reflected colour, which is how a machinist checks a surface.
UI: A sky gradient behind the hand, and white sheets with 28pt corners and a soft lift. The three surfaces are three sheets stacked at the foot: the open one in front with its title in 20pt Outfit bold and a grip; the other two peek out above it by their title strips ("tap to open"), each set in a little further, and come to the front when tapped. Outfit light at 30 to 40pt for the numerals, medium for labels, sky blue as the one colour and a warm amber only near a limit. Gauges are soft 6pt rings with round caps; controls are pills, solid sky blue for the primary and white with a hairline for the rest; the effort chart is filled with the pale blue.
Different from every previous design because: the first light-blue stage, the first mirror finish and the first sky environment, the first stacked-sheet navigation. 36 was also pale blue and rounded, but flat, clinical and tab-barred with a porcelain hand; this is airy, gradient, chrome, and its navigation is paper.
Why this is the best possible UI: it is the friendliest of the twenty without being soft: the rings and pills are unambiguous, the sheets make the structure of the app visible at all times (you can see the other two surfaces waiting), and the chrome hand in daylight is the most optimistic way to show a machine.
What I would do better next time: The sky needs a horizon line for the chrome to read as chrome rather than frosted glass. The Data sheet is tall and hides the hand almost entirely. The peeking strips could show a live number each.

---- DESIGN 44: Cutaway ----
Thesis: A section through the mechanism: a clipping plane opens the hand so the slides and pins inside are visible, readings are engineering callouts with leader lines, and vertical section tabs down the left edge are the navigation.
Twin: The Stage learned to enable local clipping. One world plane, facing the camera and set 0.012 units behind the hand's centre, removes the near half of every member, so the palm block, the knuckle blocks and the lower finger members are opened and the telescopic slides and the encoder mounts inside are seen. Members matte pale grey-blue, double sided so the hollow interiors read as shells; encoder boards cyan and self-lit; pins orange and self-lit; navy stage, a cool key from the front, a warm rim from behind. The hand does not sway, because a section plane through a moving object is noise; dragging it rotates it through the plane, which is the one interaction that shows more. It reads as a precise instrument because a cutaway is how a mechanism is explained: you see the parts that do the work.
UI: Navy, IBM Plex Mono. Readings are callouts: a boxed label with a 1pt cyan leader to a dot, placed at the four corners of the drawing: mean flexion, effort, the peak finger, the live pins. The left edge is a rail of vertical section tabs, A-A LIVE, B-B REPLAY, C-C DATA, in 104pt strips with the text rotated; the active one is solid cyan with navy text. Commands are boxed with a small cyan tick and invert to cyan when pressed. Fingers are 6pt bars; the twelve joints are 17pt numerals over their own bars; the transport is a ruled trace with an orange playhead and a row of small boxed commands.
Different from every previous design because: the only twin with an interior; the only vertical-tab navigation; the only navy-cyan-orange palette; the only design whose readings connect to the drawing with leaders. 33 and 34 also used a technical mono and callout-like framing, but on blue line work and green holograms respectively, with sheet tabs and a dial.
Why this is the best possible UI: the whole product story is "a real mechanism, faithfully driven", and a cutaway proves it in one look. The callouts put the numbers where an engineer would write them, on the drawing, and the section tabs make the app read as the drawing set it is.
What I would do better next time: The cut interiors are dark; a light from inside the section plane, or a brighter double-sided fill, would show the slides better. Leaders should end on the real projected joint positions, which needs the Stage to expose projections. The welcome's callout says little.
